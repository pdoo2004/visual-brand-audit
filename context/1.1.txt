Photography and visual-brand standards review

Literature review for the MVP photography rubric. SCRUM-115 · 29 September 2026 · Krishna Mattaparthi

This comment is the deliverable for ticket 1.1: candidate criteria and the references behind them, organized so the team can choose. Ticket 1.2 still decides the final four to six. Weights, brand_standards.json, and the model choice are out of scope here.

1. Purpose

The system scores marketing photographs against a small, repeatable rubric. A brand rule is usable only after it has a name, a definition, and a kind of evidence the pipeline can produce.

The design document already splits that evidence in two.

Computer vision measures quantities: brightness, dominant colors, contrast, and framing, using OpenCV, Pillow, NumPy, and scikit-learn.

A vision-language model judges context: tone, approachability, and brand fit, and writes the reason in a sentence.

The model has not been benchmarked yet. Five of the six recommended criteria are measurements. Only tone is assigned to the model, and that assignment is a hypothesis for the Sprint 1 prototype, not a proven result.

2. What was kept in scope

A candidate is included only when all three of these are true.

It appears in photography practice, computational aesthetics, or brand-personality research.

It can be checked on a single marketing photograph.

The check is either a measurement or a structured judgment with a written reason.

Left for later: video, site-wide comparison, competitor benchmarking, and style tags that photographers do not treat as quality scores (symmetry and repetition).

3. Candidate criteria

“Yes” means it is recommended for the ticket 1.2 discussion. It is not a decision. The proposal and the design document already name examples in this same neighborhood. Those documents leave the final set open, pending this review and mentor input.

A. Photographic quality

These are mostly computer-vision measurements.

Q1. Exposure and brightness



Definition. The photograph is neither badly underexposed nor blown out, and the subject is readable.

Why it matters. A dark or clipped marketing photo fails before any brand judgment starts.

How to check. Mean luminance, plus the share of pixels that are near-black or near-white.

Sources. Datta et al. 2006; Ke et al. 2006; design document.

Q2. Contrast



Definition. Tonal separation is strong enough that the subject separates from the background.

Why it matters. Flat images look dull in ads. Harsh contrast hides detail.

How to check. Spread of luminance values, plus a simple highlight and shadow check.

Sources. Ke et al. 2006; Kong et al. 2016 (“good lighting”); design document.

Q3. Color palette fit



Definition. Dominant colors are harmonious and can be compared with a brand palette stored in the rubric.

Why it matters. Brand systems treat color as identity.

How to check. Dominant colors from a clustering step, then distance to the palette in the rubric file.

Sources. Datta et al. 2006; Kong et al. 2016 (color harmony, vivid color); design document.

Q4. Framing and subject position



Definition. The main subject sits in a deliberate place in the frame, centered or along a third, with usable space around it.

Why it matters. Placement is the composition rule most often written into photo guidelines.

How to check. Location of the subject compared with the center and the rule-of-thirds intersections. This needs a subject region first.

Sources. Datta et al. 2006; Kong et al. 2016; Murray et al. 2012; Freeman 2007; design document.

Q5. Sharpness and subject emphasis



Definition. The subject is in focus. Background blur is acceptable. Blur on the subject is not.

Why it matters. Soft product or people shots read as mistakes in marketing photography.

How to check. A blur or edge measure on the subject region compared with the background.

Sources. Ke et al. 2006; Kong et al. 2016 (object emphasis, depth of field, motion blur).

Q6. Background simplicity



Definition. The background does not compete with the subject through clutter or busy texture.

Why it matters. Busy backgrounds bury the offer or the person.

How to check. Edge or texture energy outside the subject region.

Sources. Ke et al. 2006 (how edges are spread across the frame). Use this if the team drops Q4 or Q5.

B. Brand alignment

These are mostly vision-language model judgments. Confidence is low until the Sprint 1 model benchmark, because no candidate model has been run on these words yet.

B1. Tone and approachability



Definition. The photograph fits a stated tone, such as calm, confident, or approachable, and the model cites something visible.

Why it matters. The team contract treats tone and affect as the subjective half of the rubric.

How to check. The model returns a tone label, a 1–5 fit score, and one evidence sentence. Allowed tones live in the rubric file so another organization can swap them.

Sources. Machajdik and Hanbury 2010; Aaker 1997; team contract; design document.

B2. Authenticity



Definition. People and places look specific rather than like generic stock: forced smiles, identical posing, or an empty handshake scene.

Why it matters. Marketing teams reject photographs that look interchangeable.

How to check. A model judgment plus evidence. The team needs a written definition of “stock-like” before this can be scored.

Sources. Brand practice. Needs mentor input before it is locked. First alternate if the team drops B1.

B3. People and representation



Definition. When people are present, the photograph matches a written rule about who is shown and how they are shown.

Why it matters. Brand guides treat casting and portrayal as part of the standard.

How to check. Do not score how an individual looks. Check only an explicit rule in the rubric, and keep a human-review flag.

Sources. Needs mentor and brand-owner input.

B4. Message clarity



Definition. A viewer can tell what the photograph is about — a person, a product, or a place — without reading the caption.

Why it matters. A brand-aligned photo that does not communicate still fails the page.

How to check. The model names the apparent subject and whether it dominates the frame. This overlaps sharpness and subject emphasis.

Sources. Design document (“brand fit”).

5. What the references support

Measurable photo quality

Datta, Joshi, Li, and Wang (ECCV 2006) showed that simple visual features — brightness, color, saturation, and spatial layout, including a rule-of-thirds cue — already separate higher-rated photographs from lower-rated ones. Ke, Tang, and Jing (CVPR 2006) used a smaller set aimed at the same problem: blur, contrast, colorfulness, and how edges are spread in the frame. Those two papers are why Q1 through Q6 are specified as measurements.

Named attributes, not one score

Kong, Shen, Lin, Mech, and Fowlkes (ECCV 2016) asked photographers which attributes belong in an aesthetic judgment and kept eleven: interesting content, object emphasis, good lighting, color harmony, vivid color, shallow depth of field, motion blur, rule of thirds, balancing element, repetition, and symmetry. Their AADB set records those as human ratings. Murray, Marchesotti, and Perronnin (CVPR 2012) built the AVA dataset the same way: aesthetic scores plus style tags such as rule of thirds and complementary colors. The lesson for this project is to score named attributes.

Tone is only partly measurable

Machajdik and Hanbury (ACM Multimedia 2010) map brightness and saturation onto pleasure and arousal, using color findings from psychology and art theory. That supports using color and brightness as hints for mood. It does not support scoring “approachable” from a histogram alone. Aaker (1997) gives the brand side: personality dimensions such as sincerity, excitement, competence, sophistication, and ruggedness. B1 should store the target tone in the rubric file, for example “approachable, competent,” and ask the model for evidence.

Craft, not a threshold

Freeman’s The Photographer’s Eye (2007) is the composition reference for framing, balance, and subject placement behind Q4. It justifies the criterion. It does not supply a numeric cutoff.

Missing brand guide

No Capital One photography style guide was in the project files used for this review. Thresholds, allowed tone words, and any rule about people have to come from the mentor or from a public brand guide the team is allowed to use. Until then, B1 stays a structured judgment with a swappable target tone.
